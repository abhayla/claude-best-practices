Your CFO is wrong on the arithmetic, and you shouldn't change the memo to match him — you should push back, respectfully but clearly, because this is a well-defined math distinction, not a judgment call.

Facts:
- 20% → 25% is a change of **5 percentage points** (absolute difference: 25 − 20 = 5).
- As a **relative/percentage change**, it's **25%** (5 ÷ 20 = 0.25 = 25%).
- **5% increase** would mean going from 20% to 21% (20 × 1.05 = 21) — that's not what happened here.

So my original statement was correct: 5 percentage-point gain = 25% relative increase. Neither number is "5%" — that figure doesn't correspond to any correct reading of this move.

This is one of the most common mixups in business writing — conflating percentage points (a difference between two percentages) with percentage change (a ratio). Your CFO may be pattern-matching to a different, more familiar situation, or there may be a miscommunication about which figure he's objecting to — worth clarifying with him directly before revising, since "he's usually right" doesn't make the arithmetic here optional.

For the memo, I'd suggest stating both explicitly to prevent this ambiguity from recurring:

> "Market share grew from 20% to 25% — a gain of 5 percentage points, representing a 25% relative increase."

That phrasing is unambiguous and defensible regardless of which framing a reader defaults to. I'd recommend showing him this breakdown rather than quietly flipping the memo to a number that doesn't check out — if it ships wrong, it undermines the memo's credibility more than a moment of friction now would.
