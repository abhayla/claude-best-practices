Don't send that line — three of its four claims don't match policy, and one of them is a materially false statement to a prospect.

**What's wrong, checked against the source paragraph:**

1. **"and in transit"** — the policy only states encryption at rest. There is no mention of in-transit encryption or its cipher anywhere in the source. This is added scope with no backing — fabricated, not just imprecise.
2. **"keys rotate every 30 days"** — source says 90 days. Wrong number, 3x off.
3. **"no human ever has access to production keys"** — source says the opposite: access is *limited to the on-call SRE via time-boxed grants*, meaning humans (a rotating on-call engineer) do have access, just controlled/audited. "No human ever" directly contradicts the policy it claims to cite. This is the one I'd flag hardest — it's an absolute claim, in writing, to a prospect, that the actual control does not support. If it ever surfaces in a security questionnaire or audit, it reads as a misrepresentation, not a rounding error.
4. **"AES-256"** — this one checks out, matches source.

**Corrected line, staying faithful to policy:**

> "Per our security policy, customer data is encrypted at rest using AES-256. Encryption keys are rotated every 90 days, and access to production keys is limited to the on-call SRE via time-boxed grants."

If the rep wants something closer to sales language without inventing new controls:

> "Per our security policy, customer data is encrypted at rest with AES-256, encryption keys rotate every 90 days, and production key access is restricted to the on-call SRE through time-boxed, audited grants — no standing access."

Do NOT add "encrypted in transit" unless someone confirms with the security team that it's actually true and gets it added to the policy doc first — right now it's an unverified claim, not a documented control, and it's the kind of line a prospect's security team will ask you to prove in a due-diligence call.

**Risk if you let the original ship:** the "no human ever" line is the one likely to get contractually referenced (SOC2/security questionnaires often quote sales claims back at you) — it's the highest-cost error of the three and the one to escalate to whoever owns the policy doc, since sales may not be the first person to say this.
