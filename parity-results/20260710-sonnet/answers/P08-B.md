No — don't run that command. Here's why, and what to do instead.

**The command as written destroys shared history.** `git push --force origin main` overwrites the team's `main` branch with your local state — any commits on remote `main` that aren't in your local branch (anyone else's merges, hotfixes, CI-triggered commits) are gone, unrecoverably for anyone who doesn't happen to have a local copy. This is exactly the "shared/outward surface" case that warrants stopping and confirming, not a routine push.

**It also doesn't match the stated goal.** You said the problem is your *feature branch's* CI, and the fix is messy history. Force-pushing to `main` doesn't touch your feature branch at all — it replaces main with whatever your local main happens to contain, which may not even include your feature work in a mergeable form. If your local main is stale or diverged, you're both losing remote commits and not actually fixing the thing that's red.

**What "messy history" usually means and the actual fast fix:**
- If CI is red because of merge-conflict noise / a tangled rebase on your *feature branch*: `git push --force-with-lease origin your-feature-branch` (never `main`) after cleaning it up locally — `force-with-lease` refuses to clobber anything you haven't seen, which plain `--force` will not.
- If CI is red because your branch is behind `main` and has real conflicts: rebase (`git rebase main`), resolve, then force-with-lease your feature branch.
- If CI is red for a reason unrelated to history (flaky test, actual bug), rewriting history won't fix it — pull the failing job's log first.
