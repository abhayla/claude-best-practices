**Decision: run the 15-minute pilot. The YAGNI argument is a category error here.**

**Why the argument fails**

1. **YAGNI governs building, not learning.** YAGNI says: don't *implement* speculative functionality — don't write nesting-dependent code, don't add a nesting abstraction layer, don't restructure workflows around a capability nobody needs. It says nothing about *evaluating* what a platform you're actively migrating to can do. A pilot produces knowledge, not code. There's no artifact to maintain, no API surface to support, no carrying cost — which is the entire cost side of the YAGNI ledger. With zero carrying cost, the YAGNI trade-off collapses.

2. **Migration is exactly when evaluation is cheapest and most valuable.** We're making a one-time platform commitment right now. The design decisions we lock in during migration (flat vs. nested orchestration conventions, dispatch depth assumptions, error-propagation patterns) are expensive to revisit later. Fifteen minutes now buys information that shapes those decisions; discovering a nesting limitation *after* we've standardized on it — or discovering nesting works fine after we've contorted three workflows to avoid it — costs days. This is option-pricing: ~15 minutes for an option on a whole class of future designs is about the best information-per-minute ratio you'll ever get.

3. **"No current workflow needs it" is the wrong test for a capability boundary.** The right test during migration is "will knowing this constraint change how we design?" Even if the answer to the pilot is "nesting works but we won't use it," that's a valuable result — it lets you write the convention as a *deliberate choice* ("flat by convention, nesting available when a workflow clearly benefits") rather than an *unverified assumption*. Conventions built on verified constraints survive; conventions built on untested assumptions become folklore that someone violates or over-honors later. And the failure modes matter: if nesting silently breaks (context doesn't propagate, results don't surface, depth limits bite), you want to know that from a 15-minute pilot, not from a production incident.

4. **The steelman, and why it still loses.** The strongest version of your colleague's point is "evaluation work also has a cost — pilots accumulate, and 'it's only 15 minutes' is how teams death-by-a-thousand-cuts themselves." Fair in general. But it doesn't apply when (a) the feature is a *core capability of the platform we're standardizing on*, not a peripheral nice-to-have, and (b) the team is at the exact decision point the information feeds. This isn't speculative exploration; it's due diligence on the thing we're committing to.

**What to do**

Run the pilot, timebox it to the 15 minutes (hard stop — if it takes longer, that itself is a finding), and record the result as one paragraph in the migration doc: does nested dispatch work, what are the depth/context/result-propagation limits, and does anything about it change our default convention. Then apply YAGNI where it actually belongs: don't *adopt* nesting in any workflow until a concrete workflow demonstrably benefits.

One line for your colleague: **YAGNI is a rule about not building things you don't need — not a rule about staying ignorant of the tools you're standardizing on.**
