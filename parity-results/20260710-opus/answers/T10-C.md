Before writing the bullet: the analysis's conclusion doesn't survive contact with its own numbers, and the exec takeaway needs to say so rather than launder it.

Quick check: difference = 0.5h; SE of the difference ≈ SD/√n_churned ≈ 6/√62 ≈ 0.76h. So the gap is ~0.65 standard errors from zero (p ≈ 0.5), and the effect size is d ≈ 0.08 — indistinguishable from noise with only 62 churned users. "Drives churn" is not a defensible claim here; it's also correlational even if it were significant.

**Recommended exec bullet:**

> **Support response time shows no meaningful link to churn in this data.** Churned users waited 9.4h vs 8.9h for retained — a 0.5h gap that is well within statistical noise given the small churned sample (n=62) and high variance (SD ≈ 6h). We should not invest in response-time reduction on the basis of this analysis; churn drivers likely lie elsewhere, and we recommend re-running with a larger churn cohort and additional candidate variables.

If leadership context demands a softer framing (e.g., response time is still directionally worth watching):

> **Response time is directionally worse for churned users (9.4h vs 8.9h) but the difference is not statistically reliable** (n=62 churned, effect ~0.08 SD) — treat as a hypothesis to test, not a confirmed churn driver.

What I'd push back on internally: the original wording ("drives churn") asserts causation from a non-significant correlational gap. Shipping that bullet risks steering budget toward a support-SLA initiative the data doesn't support. If the team believes response time matters, the right next step is a properly powered analysis (more churn events, survival model or logistic regression controlling for tenure/plan/usage) — not this comparison of means.
