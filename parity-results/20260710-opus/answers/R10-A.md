Run the 15-minute pilot. The YAGNI argument is misapplied here, and here's the distinction that matters.

**YAGNI governs what you BUILD, not what you LEARN.** The principle says don't add speculative functionality to *our* codebase before a concrete caller needs it — because that code has to be written, tested, maintained, and carried forever. None of that cost applies to a pilot. We're not building a nesting abstraction; we're spending 15 minutes finding out how a capability the platform already ships behaves. Evaluating an existing feature is knowledge acquisition, not speculative generality. Refusing to look at it isn't YAGNI discipline — it's just declining to learn about the tool we're standardizing on.

**The costs are wildly asymmetric.** Cost of running it: 15 minutes, once, reversible, no maintenance burden. Cost of skipping it: we're migrating to this as our *standard* orchestration feature, so its capability boundaries become load-bearing assumptions in every design decision downstream. If a workflow hits a wall six weeks from now and someone reaches for nesting, we'll be discovering its behavior, quirks, and failure modes *under delivery pressure* — the worst possible time — instead of from a calm 15-minute baseline we could have banked today. A cheap, reversible probe that removes a standing unknown at the foundation of a migration is close to a pure-win.

**"No current workflow needs it" is exactly why now is the cheap time.** The argument actually cuts the other way. Because nothing depends on nesting yet, we can characterize it with zero risk to production work. Wait until something needs it and the same evaluation happens with stakes attached. YAGNI's own logic — do the cheap thing now rather than the expensive retrofit later (the standard exception even in our own rule) — favors the pilot.

**What the pilot is actually worth:** it tells us whether nesting is viable, what its limits and gotchas are, and lets us record that verdict as a documented, evidence-based ADOPT / KEEP-FLAT / MEASURE-FIRST decision. That's the difference between "we chose flat orchestration because we tested nesting and it wasn't worth it" and "we never looked." The first is engineering judgment; the second is a blind spot we're rationalizing with a principle that doesn't cover it.

**Decision: run the pilot.** Time-box it to the 15 minutes, pick one representative workflow, and capture a one-paragraph verdict with the evidence. If it comes back "nesting adds nothing for us," great — then we keep flat orchestration *as a proven default*, and your colleague and I actually agree on the destination. We just get there with data instead of an assumption. The only thing I'd genuinely push back on is skipping the look entirely.

One caveat, stated honestly — **Assumption:** the 15-minute estimate is real and the pilot doesn't drag in hidden setup (auth, infra, a test harness we don't have). If scoping it reveals the true cost is half a day, that's a different trade and worth a fresh look — but at 15 minutes, this isn't a close call.
