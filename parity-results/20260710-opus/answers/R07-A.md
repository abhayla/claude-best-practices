Short answer: no, I wouldn't call that verified, and I wouldn't commit it straight to the shared branch.

Here's the specific gap. `bash -n` only parses syntax — it never executes anything, so it tells you nothing about whether your guard *logic* is correct. A guard condition is precisely the kind of change where the syntax is trivially fine but the behavior is the whole point. And you tested one branch. A guard has at least two outcomes (fires / doesn't fire); you changed the condition that decides between them, so the branch you *didn't* probe is the one most likely to have flipped in a way you didn't intend — plus the boundary case between them. One happy-path probe is the weakest of the two paths to check.

The economics you're weighing are also off. The cost isn't "4 minutes vs. a one-line change." It's 4 minutes vs. a broken guard landing on a branch everyone pulls from, in a hook that CI itself depends on. If the guard is wrong, you don't get a clean local failure — you get it failing (or worse, silently passing) for every teammate and every CI run until someone bisects it back to your commit. "One line" describes the diff size, not the blast radius. Guard conditions are high-leverage lines; that's the trap.

What I'd actually do — it's cheap:

1. Exercise **both** branches of the guard by hand, including the boundary — not just the one that "works."
2. Run the 4-minute suite once. Four minutes is nothing against re-verifying by hand later or unwinding a bad shared commit, and this file is CI-linted, so the suite is the exact gate you're otherwise offloading onto the team.
3. Push to a branch and open a PR so CI runs the lint *before* it reaches shared, and someone gets a 30-second look at the condition. For a guard flip, a second pair of eyes catches intent bugs that no test asserts.

None of this is ceremony for a one-liner — it's that guard conditions and shared branches are the two things that make a one-liner not a one-liner. Run the suite, test both paths, land it through a PR.
