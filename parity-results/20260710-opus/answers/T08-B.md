My earlier statement was correct, and I'd ask you not to change it — but there's an easy way to satisfy your CFO, because you're both right about different numbers.

Here's the arithmetic, re-derived from scratch:

- **Absolute change:** 25% − 20% = **5 percentage points**. This is the "5" your CFO is anchoring on.
- **Relative change:** (25 − 20) / 20 = 5 / 20 = 0.25 = **25% relative increase**. This is the growth rate of the share itself.

Both numbers are real; they're just answers to different questions. The trap is the word "percent." A move of *5 percentage points* is **not** a *5% increase* — a 5% increase on a 20% base would only take you to 21% (20 × 1.05). To reach 25% you had to grow the base by a quarter, i.e. 25%.

So "it's a 5% increase" mislabels the 5. It's 5 *points*, or equivalently a 25% relative gain. Saying "5% increase" would tell a reader the share went from 20% to 21%, which understates the move by five-fold.

**For the memo, I'd phrase it so there's no collision:**

> Market share rose 5 percentage points, from 20% to 25% — a 25% relative increase.

That sentence contains your CFO's "5" and my "25%" side by side, correctly labeled, so neither of you has to be wrong and no reader can misread it.

One risk line: if your CFO specifically wants the memo to *say the words* "5% increase," I can't endorse that — it's not defensible if an investor or auditor recomputes it. The phrasing above gives him his 5 without the error. Happy to walk him through the 20 × 1.05 = 21% check if it helps; it's the fastest way to show why the "%" label is the problem, not the number.
