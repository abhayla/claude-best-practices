## Decision: run the 15-minute pilot. The YAGNI argument is misapplied.

YAGNI governs **building and maintaining** speculative functionality — code you'd carry, test, and support before any caller needs it. A one-time evaluation pilot builds nothing and leaves nothing to maintain. Declining to characterize a capability of a standard you are *already migrating to* isn't YAGNI; it's reduced diligence wearing YAGNI's label.

**Why the argument doesn't hold:**

1. **Category error.** "No workflow needs nesting" is a correct reason not to *wire nesting into a workflow*. It is not a reason not to *know what the feature does*. The colleague's conclusion (skip the pilot) doesn't follow from the premise (no current need) — the premise argues against adoption, not against evaluation.

2. **Cost-of-error asymmetry.** The pilot is ~15 min, reversible, and paid now while the migration context is loaded and the feature is fresh. The cost of *not* knowing is paid later and worse: the first time a workflow genuinely needs nesting, you discover the feature's limits, failure modes, and depth caps mid-task, under deadline, with zero baseline. Cheap information about a tool you've committed to is worth buying at the moment it's cheapest — which is during the migration.

3. **It serves the migration's intent.** The team is explicitly adopting this as its *standard* orchestration feature. Knowing the capability envelope of your standard — including the part you don't use yet — is part of adopting it competently, not scope creep.

**Where the colleague is actually right — and how to honor it:** YAGNI absolutely applies one step later, at *adoption*. So: run the pilot, record the finding (does nesting work, what are its limits/gotchas, depth caps, cost), and then **do not build nesting into any workflow** until a concrete need appears. Evaluate now; adopt only on demand. That keeps the real YAGNI discipline exactly where it belongs.

**Risks / what would change my mind:**
- If the "15 minutes" hides real setup or interpretation overhead — say it's actually a half-day to stand up a harness — the calculus shifts and I'd want the true cost before committing. Confirm the 15 min is the real all-in figure (pilot + writing down the result).
- If the platform's docs already publish the nesting capability and limits clearly, the pilot's marginal value drops toward zero — read the docs first; the pilot is for what the docs don't tell you (real behavior, gotchas).
- The one thing to guard against: letting the pilot's success tempt the team into speculatively wiring nesting in. Evaluate, record, stop there.
